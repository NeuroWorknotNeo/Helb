\hintstitle{Integrals -- 4. Series and integrals}{Class work}

\problem{1}
\hint{1}
Write \(x^{-x}=e^{-x\ln x}=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-x\ln x)^n}{n!}\) and \(x^x=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(x\ln x)^n}{n!}\). You need \(\int_0^1x^n\ln^nx\,dx\).
\hint{2}
Substituting \(x=e^{-u}\): \(\int_0^1x^n\ln^nx\,dx=(-1)^n\int_0^\infty u^ne^{-(n+1)u}du=\frac{(-1)^nn!}{(n+1)^{n+1}}\). Plugging in gives \(\sum_{n\ge0}(n+1)^{-(n+1)}\) and \(\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^n(n+1)^{-(n+1)}\).
\hint{3}
Justification: for \(x^{-x}\) all terms are \(\ge0\) on \((0,1]\) (since \(-x\ln x\ge0\)), so monotone convergence applies; for \(x^x\) the series is dominated by \(e^{\lvert x\ln x\rvert}\le e^{1/e}\). Numerically \(\int_0^1x^{-x}dx=1+\frac14+\frac1{27}+\frac1{256}+\dots\approx1.291\).

\problem{2}
\hint{1}
For \(x\gt0\), \(\frac1{e^x-1}=\sum_{k\ge1}e^{-kx}\), and \(\int_0^\infty x^{s-1}e^{-kx}dx=\frac{\Gamma(s)}{k^s}\) (substitute \(u=kx\)).
\hint{2}
For real \(s\gt1\) the terms are nonnegative, so summation and integration commute (monotone convergence); for complex \(s\) with \(\operatorname{Re}s=\sigma\gt1\) dominate by the case \(s=\sigma\). Then \(s=2\): \(\Gamma(2)\zeta(2)=\frac{\pi^2}6\); \(s=4\): \(\Gamma(4)\zeta(4)=6\cdot\frac{\pi^4}{90}=\frac{\pi^4}{15}\).

\problem{3}
\hint{1}
All four are geometric series or derivatives of one: (a) \(\sum_n\binom{n+3}{3}x^n=\frac1{(1-x)^4}\); (b) \(\sum_{k\ge1}(-1)^kx^{2k}=-\frac{x^2}{1+x^2}\); (c) \(\sin\frac{k\pi}2\) runs through \(0,1,0,-1,\dots\), so the sum is \(x-x^3+x^5-\dots=\frac{x}{1+x^2}\); (d) \(\frac{x^2}{1-x}\).
\hint{2}
Answers: (a) \(\left[\frac1{3(1-x)^3}\right]_0^{1/2}=\frac73\); (b) \(\frac\pi4-1\); (c) \(\frac12\ln(1+x^2)+C\) (for \(\lvert x\rvert\lt1\), where the series converges); (d) \(\ln2-\frac58\). In (b) the series diverges at \(x=1\), but it converges on \([0,1)\) and the closed form is integrable, so the integral is understood as the integral of the sum there.

\problem{4}
\hint{1}
Multiply out \((1+x)(1+x^2)(1+x^4)\cdots(1+x^{2^{m}})\): by uniqueness of binary expansions every power \(x^j\) with \(0\le j\lt 2^{m+1}\) appears exactly once.
\hint{2}
So \(\prod_{k\ge0}(1+x^{2^k})=\frac1{1-x}\) for \(0\le x\lt1\) (equivalently, the finite product equals \(\frac{1-x^{2^{m+1}}}{1-x}\)). The integrand is \(1-x\) and the integral is \(\frac12\).

\problem{5}
\hint{1}
Expand \(\ln(1-x)=-\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{x^n}n\) resp. \(\frac1{1-x}=\sum_{n\ge0}x^n\); all terms have constant sign on \((0,1)\), so termwise integration is justified. Use \(\int_0^1x^n\ln x\,dx=-\frac1{(n+1)^2}\) and \(\int_0^1x^n\ln^2x\,dx=\frac2{(n+1)^3}\).
\hint{2}
First: \(\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n(n+1)^2}=\sum\left(\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}-\frac1{(n+1)^2}\right)=1-(\zeta(2)-1)=2-\frac{\pi^2}6\). Second: \(2\zeta(3)\).
