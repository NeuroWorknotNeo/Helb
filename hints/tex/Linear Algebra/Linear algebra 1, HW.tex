\hintstitle{Linear algebra -- 1. Determinants and invertibility}{Homework}

\problem{1}
\hint{1}
\(B=A-J\), where \(J\) is the all-ones matrix, which has rank \(1\). Show that \(\operatorname{rank}B^3\lt 10\) (or \(\operatorname{rank} B\lt 10\)).
\hint{2}
Expand \(B^3=(A-J)^3\). The term \(A^3\) vanishes, and each of the remaining \(7\) terms contains a factor \(J\), so has rank at most \(1\). Hence \(\operatorname{rank}B^3\le 7\lt 10\), so \(\det B^3=0\).

\problem{2}
\hint{1}
Expand \((A+B)(A^{-1}+B^{-1})=E\): you get \(AB^{-1}+BA^{-1}=-E\). Put \(X=AB^{-1}\); then \(BA^{-1}=X^{-1}\).
\hint{2}
\(X+X^{-1}=-E\) means \(X^2+X+E=O\), hence \(X^3-E=(X-E)(X^2+X+E)=O\). So \((\det X)^3=1\), and over \(\mathbb R\) this gives \(\det X=1\), i.e.\ \(\det A=\det B\).
\hint{3}
Over \(\mathbb C\) the determinant of \(X\) can be a non-real cube root of unity. For \(1\times1\) blocks, \(\alpha^{-1}+1=(\alpha+1)^{-1}\) iff \(\alpha^2+\alpha+1=0\). So with \(\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}\), \(B=E\) and \(A\) diagonal with entries in \(\{\omega,\omega^2\}\) satisfy the relation; take \(A=\omega E\) if \(3\nmid n\) and \(A=\operatorname{diag}(\omega,\dots,\omega,\omega^2)\) if \(3\mid n\). Then \(\det A\ne1=\det B\): the answer is no, for every \(n\ge2\).

\problem{3}
\hint{1}
Write \(M=\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\y&z\end{pmatrix}\). Then \(a^2-4b=(x+z)^2-4(xz-y^2)=(x-z)^2+(2y)^2\).
\hint{2}
Since \(x+z=a\) is fixed, \(M\) is determined by the point \((x-z,2y)\), and the admissible points form the circle of radius \(\sqrt{a^2-4b}\). So there is no \(M\) if \(a^2\lt4b\), infinitely many if \(a^2\gt4b\), and exactly one (\(M=\tfrac a2E\)) if \(a^2=4b\). Answer: exactly the pairs with \(a^2=4b\).

\problem{4}
\hint{1}
\(2)\Rightarrow1)\) is immediate (take \(B_i=A_i\)). For the converse, use that \(A_i\) is symmetric: \(\langle(\sum A_i^2)x,x\rangle=\sum\lVert A_ix\rVert^2\).
\hint{2}
Take \(x\ne0\) in the kernel of \(\sum A_i^2\); then \(A_ix=0\) for every \(i\). So \(x\) is in the kernel of \(\sum B_i^TA_i=\bigl(\sum A_iB_i\bigr)^T\) for any \(B_i\).

\problem{5}
\hint{1}
\(S=\sum A_i^TA_i\) is symmetric and \(\langle Sx,x\rangle=\sum\lVert A_ix\rVert^2\ge0\). What does this say about its eigenvalues?
\hint{2}
\(S\) is real symmetric, so its eigenvalues are real; if \(Sx=\lambda x\) with \(x\ne0\), then \(\lambda\lVert x\rVert^2=\langle Sx,x\rangle\ge0\), so \(\lambda\ge0\). Hence \(\det S\), the product of the eigenvalues, is \(\ge0\).

\problem{6}
\hint{1}
\(A\) is singular, so \(\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{adj}A\le1\).
\hint{2}
For a matrix \(C\) of rank \(\le1\), write \(C=uv^T\) and use the rank-one update formula: \(\det(E+C)=1+v^Tu=1+\operatorname{tr}C\). Apply it to \(C=\operatorname{adj}A\).

\problem{7}
\hint{1}
One direction is a computation: if \(A=B^{-1}\bar B\) then \(\bar A=\bar B^{-1}B=A^{-1}\). For the converse you need \(B\) invertible with \(BA=\bar B\).
\hint{2}
Try \(B=zE+\bar z\,\bar A\) with \(z\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}\). Using \(\bar AA=E\), check that \(BA=\bar B\).
\hint{3}
\(B=\bar z\,(\bar A+\tfrac{z}{\bar z}E)\), and \(z/\bar z\) runs over the whole unit circle, while \(\bar A\) has only finitely many eigenvalues. Choose \(z\) so that \(-z/\bar z\) is not an eigenvalue.

\problem{8}
\hint{1}
Since \(\det(AB)=\det A\det B\), it suffices to show that \(0\) is not an eigenvalue of \(AB\). Let \(S\) be the set of eigenvalues of \(AB\), and recall that \(AB\) and \(BA\) have the same characteristic polynomial, hence the same eigenvalues.
\hint{2}
The eigenvalues of \(BA=aE+bAB\) are the numbers \(a+b\mu\), \(\mu\in S\). So \(f(\mu)=a+b\mu\) maps the finite set \(S\) onto itself, i.e.\ permutes it. Hence every \(\mu\in S\) satisfies \(f^k(\mu)=\mu\) for some \(1\le k\le\lvert S\rvert\le n\).
\hint{3}
\(f^k(\mu)=b^k\mu+a(1+b+\dots+b^{k-1})\). If \(0\in S\), then \(f^k(0)=0\) gives \(a(1+b+\dots+b^{k-1})=0\). But \(a\ne0\), and since \(b\ne1\) and \(b^k\ne1\), \(1+b+\dots+b^{k-1}=\frac{b^k-1}{b-1}\ne0\), a contradiction. So \(AB\) is invertible, and so are \(A\) and \(B\). (The same computation shows that in fact every eigenvalue of \(AB\) equals \(\frac a{1-b}\).)

\problem{9}
\hint{1}
The minors of order \(n-1\) are, up to sign, the entries of \(\operatorname{adj}A\). Suppose \(\det A=0\); then \(\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{adj}A\le1\).
\hint{2}
If \(\operatorname{adj}A=O\), all \(n^2\) minors vanish. Otherwise \(\operatorname{adj}A=uv^T\) with \(u,v\ne0\). If \(u\) has \(p\) zero coordinates and \(v\) has \(q\), count the zero entries of \(uv^T\): \(np+nq-pq\). Since \(k\ge1\), \(p+q\ge1\), and this count is \(\ge n\gt k\).

\problem{10}
\hint{1}
A non-zero (real) \(X\) with \(AX=X(-A)\) exists if and only if \(A\) and \(-A\) have a common eigenvalue (Sylvester's theorem on \(AX=XB\); a complex solution gives a real one by taking real or imaginary parts). Since \(A\) is invertible, this means \(A\) has eigenvalues \(\lambda\) and \(-\lambda\) with \(\lambda\ne0\).
\hint{2}
\(\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{adj}A=e_3(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_4)\), the third elementary symmetric function of the eigenvalues. \(A^2+E\) is singular if and only if \(\pm i\) are eigenvalues, which is such a pair.
\hint{3}
Conversely, if the eigenvalues are \(\lambda,-\lambda,\mu,\nu\), compute \(e_1=\mu+\nu\) and \(e_3=-\lambda^2(\mu+\nu)\). The condition \(e_1=e_3\ne0\) forces \(\lambda^2=-1\).

\problem{11}
\hint{1}
For odd \(n\): \(\det A=\det A^T=\det(-A)=(-1)^n\det A\), so \(\det A=0\).
\hint{2}
For even \(n\) the determinant need not vanish (try \(n=2\)). The non-zero eigenvalues of a real antisymmetric matrix are purely imaginary and come in pairs \(\pm i\lambda\), so \(\det A=\prod\lambda_k^2\ge0\). In fact \(\det A\) is the square of a polynomial in the entries, the Pfaffian.

\problem{12}
\hint{1}
Use Hadamard's inequality: \(\lvert\det M\rvert\le\prod_j\lVert m_j\rVert\), where \(m_j\) are the columns of \(M\). (Proof: for invertible \(M\), Gram--Schmidt gives \(M=QR\) with \(Q\) orthogonal and \(R\) upper triangular; then \(\lvert\det M\rvert=\prod\lvert r_{jj}\rvert\) and \(\lVert m_j\rVert^2=\sum_{i\le j}r_{ij}^2\ge r_{jj}^2\).) Apply it to \(M=E+A\).
\hint{2}
The \(j\)-th column of \(E+A\) has \(\lVert m_j\rVert^2=(1+a_{jj})^2+\sum_{i\ne j}a_{ij}^2=1+x_j\) with \(x_j=2a_{jj}+\sum_ia_{ij}^2\). By \(1+x\le e^x\), \(\lVert m_j\rVert\le e^{x_j/2}\), and multiplying over \(j\) gives \(\lvert\det(E+A)\rvert\le\exp\bigl(\sum_ja_{jj}+\frac12\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}^2\bigr)=\exp\bigl(\operatorname{tr}A+\frac12\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}^2\bigr)\).

\problem{13}
\hint{1}
Subtract the first row from each of the others. What can you say about the entries of rows \(2,\dots,n\) now?
\hint{2}
They are even, so a factor \(2\) comes out of each of \(n-1\) rows. For sharpness, take the first row all ones and row \(i\) (\(i\ge2\)) equal to all ones plus \(2e_i\). After the subtraction its determinant is exactly \(2^{n-1}\).

\problem{14}
\hint{1}
Barbara wins. Look for a pairing strategy that forces two equal columns at the end.
\hint{2}
Pair column \(j\) with column \(j+1004\). Whenever Alan writes \(x\) in position \((i,j)\), Barbara writes the same \(x\) in the paired position in the same row. Check that the paired cell is always empty.

\problem{15}
\hint{1}
Player 0 wins. Player 0 places only four zeros, so aim for small zero patterns that force \(\det=0\) on their own: a zero row, a zero column, or a \(2\times2\) block of zeros (rows \(i,j\) and columns \(k,l\)). Permuting rows, permuting columns and transposing preserve these patterns and \(\lvert\det\rvert\), and they can move any cell to any other; so assume Player 1 opens at \((1,1)\).
\hint{2}
Player 0 answers at \((2,2)\). After Player 1's second move at least one of \((2,3)\), \((3,2)\) is vacant; by transposing, say \((2,3)\), and Player 0 plays there. Now Player 1 must take \((2,1)\), otherwise Player 0 plays there and row \(2\) is zero.
\hint{3}
Player 1 now holds \((1,1)\), \((2,1)\) and one more cell \(c\). Look at the square of cells in rows \(1,3\) and columns \(2,3\): two zeros of it in one column complete a zero column (with row \(2\)), and two zeros of it in one row complete a zero \(2\times2\) block with \((2,2),(2,3)\). Player 0 puts the third zero at a cell \(z\) of this square, diagonally opposite to \(c\) if \(c\) lies in the square. Then the two neighbours of \(z\) in its row and column of the square are vacant: a double threat, and the fourth zero completes a pattern.

\problem{16}
\hint{1}
Changing entry \((i,j)\) by \(t\) changes the determinant by \(t\) times the cofactor \(C_{ij}\), since \(\det\) is linear in each entry. So you need \(\det A=0\) with every cofactor non-zero, i.e.~every entry of \(\operatorname{adj}A\) non-zero.
\hint{2}
Yes, for every \(n\). If \(\operatorname{rank}A=n-1\), then \(\operatorname{adj}A=uv^T\) with \(Au=0\) and \(v^TA=0\). Pick \(A\) whose kernel and left kernel are both spanned by \((1,\dots,1)\), e.g.~\(A=nE-J\); then \(\operatorname{adj}A\) is a non-zero multiple of \(J\). (\(n=1\) is trivial.)
